What is dark energy?

Serious leads

Why it matters

Dark energy drives the accelerating expansion of the universe. Understanding it would help tell whether the universe keeps expanding forever, and if it is confirmed to change over time, our picture of the universe would need a major rethink.

What we know

The expansion of the universe is accelerating. The cause, called dark energy, makes up about 70% of its energy content.

What blocks us

Quantum physics predicts a value some 60 to 120 orders of magnitude larger than observations allow. This is a serious crisis in physics.

Current leads

DESI surveys hint that it may change over time; Euclid and the Rubin Observatory will measure it more precisely.

In depth

Where things stand

In 1998, two teams studying distant type Ia supernovae found that the expansion of the universe is speeding up, a discovery honoured with the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics 1. Measurements of the cosmic microwave background by the Planck satellite indicate that dark energy makes up about 68% of the universe's energy content, against about 32% for matter 2. The simplest explanation is a cosmological constant: an energy of empty space that does not dilute as the universe expands 3.

Competing ideas

If dark energy is the energy of empty space, quantum physics predicts a value 60 to 120 orders of magnitude larger than the one observed 3. Alternatives include a new field whose energy changes over time, sometimes called quintessence, and changes to Einstein's theory of gravity, but a review argues that these are hard to build without fine-tuning or do not remove the underlying puzzle 3. Another proposal, linked to string theory, is that a huge number of possible values exist and that we observe a small one because only such values allow galaxies and observers 3.

What would settle it

The key question is whether the density of dark energy stays exactly constant over time, as a cosmological constant requires, or changes 4. The European Space Agency's Euclid telescope, launched in July 2023, is surveying about 14,000 square degrees of sky and measuring the shapes of more than a billion galaxies to pin this down 5. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory will film the sky for ten years, and understanding dark energy is one of its four main science goals 6.

Latest developments

In 2025, the DESI survey, using more than 14 million galaxies and quasars from three years of observations, reported that its data combined with other measurements favour a dark energy that changes over time 4. The strength of this preference ranges from 2.8 to 4.2 standard deviations depending on the supernova sample used 4.

How much will Earth warm if CO2 doubles?

Serious leads

Why it matters

Climate sensitivity indicates how fast Earth will warm for a given level of emissions. Pinning it down would clarify how much more CO2 can be emitted while meeting the Paris Agreement goals, and what local changes in heat and dryness to expect.

What we know

The IPCC puts the likely answer between 2.5 and 4 °C.

What blocks us

Clouds are the most uncertain feedback: they form through processes far smaller than global climate models can represent.

Current leads

Kilometer-scale climate models, AI climate emulators.

In depth

Where things stand

Equilibrium climate sensitivity is the long-term global warming caused by doubling the concentration of carbon dioxide above its pre-industrial level 1. The IPCC's latest assessment gives a best estimate of 3.0 °C and a 90% or greater chance that it lies between 2 and 5 °C 1. This is a significant narrowing compared with the previous assessment, which gave a likely range of 1.5 to 4.5 °C 1.

Competing ideas

Instead of relying on climate models alone, scientists now combine three largely independent lines of evidence: the understanding of individual feedback processes, the temperature record since the industrial era, and past climates 2. A 2020 assessment using this approach found a likely range of 2.6 to 3.9 °C and concluded that all three lines are difficult to reconcile with a sensitivity below 2 °C 2. Past climates provide the strongest case against high values: the cooling of the last ice age argues strongly against a sensitivity above 4.5 °C 2.

What would settle it

Clouds remain the most uncertain feedback, because they form through processes far smaller than global climate models can represent 1. Understanding has improved with better observations, and the IPCC expects low clouds over subtropical oceans to decrease as the climate warms, which would amplify the warming 1.

Latest developments

Kilometre-scale climate models aim to represent storms, ocean eddies and other small-scale processes physically instead of approximating their effect 3. The European nextGEMS project, which ran from 2021 to 2025, produced the first 30-year simulations at this scale, with an atmospheric grid of about 10 km, and its models represent subtropical stratocumulus clouds well 3.

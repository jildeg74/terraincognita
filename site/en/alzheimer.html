What causes Alzheimer's disease?

Serious leads

Why it matters

Alzheimer's is the most common form of dementia, a leading cause of death and dependency in old age that also burdens families. Knowing its cause could lead to treatments that do more than modestly slow decline.

What we know

Amyloid plaques and tau protein build up. Anti-amyloid antibodies slow decline somewhat.

What blocks us

The amyloid hypothesis doesn't explain everything, and damage starts years before symptoms.

Current leads

Neuroinflammation, a possible role for viral infections, blood biomarkers for early diagnosis.

In depth

Where things stand

An estimated 57 million people lived with dementia worldwide in 2019, a number projected to reach 153 million by 2050 1. Alzheimer's disease is defined in the brain by amyloid plaques and tau tangles, but most patients also show other kinds of damage at the same time 2. A 2024 Lancet Commission estimated that 14 modifiable risk factors, including untreated vision loss and high LDL cholesterol, account for nearly half of the risk of dementia 1.

Competing ideas

The dominant amyloid cascade hypothesis holds that amyloid build-up triggers the disease, yet 30 to 40% of cognitively normal older people have amyloid deposits in their brains 2. Critics point out that almost all treatments aimed at amyloid failed over three decades, and argue that amyloid may enter the process late rather than at the start 2. Alternative ideas focus on tau, on failures in the systems that recycle the cell's waste and proteins, on the innate immune system, and on the APOE gene, which is involved in transporting fats 2.

What would settle it

The author of a 2024 perspective argues that the amyloid cascade remains a working hypothesis, no less but no more, because the disease is still not understood well enough 2. Simpler tests reduce the need for brain scans and spinal taps: the first blood test to help diagnose Alzheimer's, cleared by the US FDA in May 2025, measures the ratio of two proteins, pTau217 and beta-amyloid 1-42, in adults aged 55 and older with symptoms 4.

Latest developments

In a 2023 trial of 1,795 people with early Alzheimer's, the antibody lecanemab, which targets amyloid, slowed decline on a clinical rating scale by 0.45 points over 18 months compared with placebo 3. Brain swelling or fluid build-up visible on scans occurred in 12.6% of treated patients 3. For the blood test cleared in 2025, 91.7% of positive results in a study of 499 adults were confirmed by a brain scan or a spinal fluid test 4.

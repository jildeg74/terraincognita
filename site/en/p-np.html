Is P equal to NP?

Distant horizon

Why it matters

It asks whether every problem whose answer is easy to check is also easy to solve. If so, transport could be scheduled optimally, moving people and goods faster and more cheaply, but public-key cryptography would become impossible.

What we know

Posed in 1971: if a problem is easy to check, is it also easy to solve?

What blocks us

Several major families of proof techniques have been proven insufficient.

Current leads

Geometric complexity theory, circuit lower bounds.

In depth

Where things stand

P versus NP asks whether every problem whose solution is easy to check is also easy to solve 1. Stephen Cook and Leonid Levin formulated it independently in 1971, and it is one of the Millennium Problems of the Clay Mathematics Institute 1. For example, checking a proposed list of students to be given dormitory places under given rules is easy, but producing such a list from scratch seems impractically hard 1.

Competing ideas

Most computer scientists conjecture that P is not equal to NP; in a 2002 poll, 61 respondents expected P to differ from NP and 9 expected them to be equal 2. If P equalled NP and the algorithm were efficient in practice, essentially all the cryptography used on the internet today would be broken 2.

What would settle it

Any proof that P differs from NP must get past three known barriers, called relativization, natural proofs and algebrization, each showing that a whole family of techniques cannot settle the question on its own 2. Geometric complexity theory, which draws on algebraic geometry and representation theory, is a very ambitious program to get past them 2.

Latest developments

Progress has come in smaller steps, such as Ryan Williams's proof that a restricted class of circuits, called ACC, cannot solve every problem in a huge class called NEXP 2. The survey presents this as an example of turning fast algorithms into proofs of limits, an approach its author calls ironic complexity theory 2.

How can we defeat antibiotic resistance?

Breakthrough in sight

Why it matters

Drug-resistant bacteria are linked to millions of deaths a year and make once-effective treatments useless. Beating resistance would keep bacterial infections treatable and could avert many deaths in the coming decades.

What we know

Resistant bacteria are associated with millions of deaths a year worldwide.

What blocks us

New antibiotics earn little, which has limited the interest of investors and most large drug companies.

Current leads

AI-discovered antibiotics such as halicin, and bacteria-killing viruses (phages).

In depth

Where things stand

Antibiotic-resistant bacteria directly caused about 1.14 million deaths in 2021 and were associated with 4.71 million 1. Since 1990, these deaths have fallen by half among children under five but risen by more than 80% among people aged 70 and over 1. Without further action, the researchers forecast 39 million deaths from resistance between 2025 and 2050 1.

Competing ideas

For new antibiotics, the World Health Organization describes a dual crisis of scarcity and lack of innovation: 90 antibacterials were in clinical development in 2025, down from 97 in 2023, and only 15 were innovative 2. Early research depends on fragile small firms, since 90% of the companies in the preclinical pipeline have fewer than 50 employees 2. Artificial intelligence offers a new route: in 2020, a neural network trained to predict antibacterial molecules led researchers to halicin, a compound structurally unlike conventional antibiotics 3.

What would settle it

Real progress means new drugs reaching patients against the most dangerous bacteria, yet only 5 agents in the 2025 clinical pipeline target the pathogens WHO ranks as critical 2. WHO warns that without more investment in research and development, drug-resistant infections will keep spreading 2.

Latest developments

In 2024, researchers described zosurabalpin, a candidate from a new antibiotic class that blocks the transport of a key building block of the outer membrane of carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii 4. The authors note that no new chemical class active against this bacterium had reached patients in over 50 years, and human trials of zosurabalpin have begun 4.
